## The promise

Every line passes two tests: anyone who has seen a "quick fix" made straight in a live system can follow it, and a platform security engineer finds nothing wrong in it. South Africa wrote its constitution in 1996 so that no side could put it into force alone: principles fixed before a word was written, which nobody could change; a text written by an elected assembly; and a court that hadn't written it, which sent it back on nine points and certified it only once all nine were fixed. Production can change the same way: rules set before the change, which the change can't loosen; a review by someone who didn't write it, of the last version pushed; a release approver's yes; and a deploy by the job's own identity, which can sign in only from the one job that waited for that yes. **Production changes only through a reviewed path, deployed by a workload identity, never by a person's hand.**

## The story in one paragraph

South Africa, 1993. The parties negotiating the end of apartheid have to agree how a new constitution will be written. In April 1994 all South Africans will vote together for the first time, and the winners will hold most of the seats in the parliament that writes it. The smaller parties fear what a winning majority could write: rights a later vote could take away, courts that answer to a party. The party expected to win won't accept a constitution written by negotiators nobody elected. So they agree 34 principles first, write them into the interim constitution, in force from 27 April 1994, and make them impossible to change: the new parliament, sitting as the Constitutional Assembly, will write the text, and it counts only once the new Constitutional Court certifies that every part keeps every principle. The Assembly adopts its text on 8 May 1996, with 86 per cent of its members; on 6 September the Court, unanimous, declines to certify it, on nine points; in five weeks the Assembly fixes all nine; on 4 December the Court certifies the amended text; it is signed on 10 December and in force from 4 February 1997, and it is still the country's constitution. At the university, the nightly job that loads the registrar's records has its own identity and a key with no secret in it, and three rooms, development, test and production. But nothing guards the way in: whatever reaches `main` in GitHub deploys straight to production, and anyone on the team can push to it. Sam sets the rules first: a ruleset on `main` nobody may bypass, code owners read from `main`, so no change picks its reviewers. Jun's change, to set an unreadable row aside and load the rest, is tried in a copy of his own, reviewed by Sam, sent back for a line that wrote students' rows to the log, fixed, and approved on its last push. Test runs it, signed in as `registrar-loader-test`; production waits for Noor Rahman's yes, which whoever started the run can't give; then the deploy signs in as `registrar-loader`, which Databricks lets in only from that one job, and people may only look at the job in production. The two actions the deploy runs are pinned to the commits Sam checked, Dependabot proposes their next versions as pull requests, and the workflow's token only reads. A week later a row is unreadable again: it's set aside, the rest is in by a quarter past two, the registrar's office fixes it by half past nine, and nobody switches on any power to look.

**Practice, the author's words in the brief:** "Production changes only through a reviewed path, deployed by a workload identity, never by a person's hand." What practitioners know, written into the film:

- *The quick fix in production* is how most paths get skipped: someone with rights fixes it live "just this once", nobody else reads it, and the next deploy overwrites it or keeps it nobody knows how. So the film's people have no rights to change production at all, only to look; the emergency path is an admin account switched on and recorded (*Power you switch on*), then the fix goes through the path.
- *Approvals that approve nothing*: a reviewer asked to approve a change they can't see, or an approval given before the last push. So the reviewer sees the change and the checks, the last push needs its own yes, and the release approver sees what changed, who reviewed it and the test run.
- *Admins bypass by default*: branch protection and environments have often let administrators skip them. So the bypass list is empty and the environment's administrator bypass is off.
- *The key on any branch*: a federation subject that names the environment only helps if the environment lets only `main` deploy and waits for a reviewer; without that, any branch's workflow that names the environment gets the key.
- *Someone else's code*: a third-party action named by a tag runs whatever the tag points to that day, beside the pipeline's secrets and keys. Pin it to a commit, keep its version beside the pin, and let a bot propose updates that people review.
- *Logs are data too*: debugging lines that print records put personal data where no mask or grant applies. Reviewers look for them.

- **The barrel organ leads.** A small pipe organ, as English parish churches had in the early 1800s, whose tunes are pinned on a wooden barrel: turning the handle works the bellows and turns the barrel, and the pins open the pipes, so whoever turns it can't change a note. Stopped wooden pipes, soft and round (odd harmonics, rolled off well below 2.5 kHz), no tremulant; a little wind as each note opens. Every note comes exactly on its pin. It's the path: what was pinned and checked is what plays. The mark is a short phrase in E-flat major, played once, exactly, where a reviewed change is released (the certified text in 1996; the merge; Noor's yes; the 02:00 run).
- **The bandora is the people who review.** The wire-strung bass of the English consort (London, 1562), plucked softly with the fingers, low and plain: each person's review one note (the Court's look, Sam's review, Noor's); two notes that agree, a fifth, where a person says yes. Never for a machine, never for a check.
- **Keys.** E-flat major, tuned pure from E-flat, for the path; C minor under the problem (the fears of 1993, the door with no gate) and under "Not certified". A held chord gives way where the next begins.
- **Music** under the opening story (*Principles first* and *Sent back*, to the title), then only at a chapter's turn, under a moment on screen (the ruleset's last tick, "Approved · the last push", Noor's yes, the lock that lights, the pins) or in a pause without words; never a bed under the voice.
