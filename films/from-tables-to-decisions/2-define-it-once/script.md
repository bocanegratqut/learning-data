## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has never heard of a semantic layer can follow it, and an analytics engineer or a BI lead who keeps one finds nothing wrong in it. *Reports on demand* let Rohan ask for casual teaching cost and get it in a minute. This film shows where that number's meaning lives. Defining a measure once is decades old, as the author says; what's new is where the definition lives, and that tools have begun to share one format for it. So casual teaching cost, counted three ways in three tools, is defined once, near the data, with its owner, its words and its rules: a Unity Catalog metric view the data project builds and reviews like code. dbt's own semantic layer, MetricFlow, does the same job in the project. And Apache Ossie, an open format with converters for each tool, lets a definition travel between them, so none of them holds it alone.

## The story in one paragraph

In 1889 a cylinder of platinum and iridium in a vault near Paris became the kilogram, and forty copies went out to the world. Every forty years or so the copies came back to be weighed against it, and by 1989 they had drifted apart by up to about fifty millionths of a gram; nobody could tell which had moved, because by definition the cylinder was always exactly one kilogram, and every exact weighing rested on one object that could be scratched or lost. In 2018 the world's conference on weights and measures voted to define the kilogram by a constant of nature instead, from May 2019, and any well-equipped laboratory can now make one: in the United States, a Kibble balance replaced copy number 20. At the university, in August, the Faculty of Health asked what casual teaching cost in Semester 1 and got three numbers from three tools: A$2.46 million from its spreadsheet (the hours in the contracts), A$1.88 million from payroll's report (the pay paid on the semester's pay days) and A$2.21 million from Finance's (the claims approved for the semester's teaching, plus on-costs). Each was right for its own question, but all three had one name, and Rohan Desai's definition, the one for this question, lived only inside his report; budgeted on payroll's number, Health would have been A$330,000 short, about one tutorial in seven with no money for a tutor. In September, Rohan defined it once, with Jun Park: its owner, its words, its rules, counted by the teaching period, and the other two numbers got names of their own. Its home is a metric view in Unity Catalog, governed like a table and read by every tool on the platform; Jun writes it as YAML in the data project, Rohan approves its meaning and Noor approves the change to production. dbt's MetricFlow does the same job, and a measure gets one home, never two. Apache Ossie lets the definition travel, with its words and rules, to any tool that reads it, through converters that say what they can't carry. Today, pay day, Genie One and the fortnightly dashboard both say A$1.84 million so far this semester, and Rohan budgets next year on the cost.

It differs from the tools' pages in one way: it doesn't pick a winner between the platform's metric view and dbt's MetricFlow, but asks for one home per measure, near most of its readers, with the open format between them. Best way there is just go to official documentation and implement it." ## Rigour to check before recording

Checked on 5 November 2026; the sources are listed below. Product names are as their docs give them that day. No release status is shown or said; the statuses read are in *Sources* only.

- **The cylinder, 1889.** The BIPM: "The 1889 definition of the kilogram was simply the mass of the international prototype of the kilogram, an artefact made of platinum-iridium"; the first CGPM declared that "this prototype shall henceforth be considered to be the unit of mass" (*History of the SI: the kilogram*). It is about 39 mm tall and wide, 90% platinum and 10% iridium. It is kept at the BIPM, in the Pavillon de Breteuil, whose address is Sèvres, near Paris ("a basement vault"; NIST: "a triple-locked vault on the outskirts of Paris"). Two nested bell jars are drawn: the sources agree on "two or more nested bell jars", not on how many.
- **Forty copies.** BIPM: "Forty similar prototypes were made at about the same time", and "most of these 'national prototypes' were individually assigned to Member States". "Each country checks its weights against its copy": each national standard of mass was its copy, and weights were traced to it.
- **About every forty years.** "About once every 40 years, the national prototypes were compared with and recalibrated against the international prototype" (Wikipedia, *2019 revision of the SI*, citing the BIPM). The pills are the verifications' years as the BIPM gives them: the first, 1889; "the second verification of national prototypes in 1946"; "the third verification carried out from 1989 to 1991".
- **The drift.** The BIPM: "the median difference being about 25 micrograms for the set of original prototypes"; the IPK's own page: "the IPK lost perhaps 50 μg of mass over the last century, and possibly significantly more, in comparison to its official copies". The film says "up to about fifty millionths of a gram", the larger of the two, as *Keeping it true*'s rigour and the module's `openings.md` have it. The fan is drawn, not plotted from data.
- **Nobody can tell which moved.** "There was no way of determining whether the national prototypes were gaining mass or whether the IPK was losing mass" (*2019 revision of the SI*); "Before 2019, by definition, the error in the measured value of the IPK's mass was exactly zero; the mass of the IPK was the kilogram" (*International Prototype of the Kilogram*). "Could be scratched, or lost": the standards are "prone to scratches and other wear", and cleaning removes "between 5 and 60 μg of contamination"; NIST: "After 1960, the kilogram was the only SI unit still defined in terms of a single manufactured object."
- **The vote and the constant.** The 26th CGPM (13 to 16 November 2018) adopted the revised SI, unanimously; the kilogram is "defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the Planck constant h to be 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ when expressed in the unit J s" (BIPM, *The kilogram*), in force from 20 May 2019. *Keeping it true* already cites the vote on 16 November 2018. The film says "the world's conference on weights and measures" for the CGPM and gives no vote count: the sources differ.
- **Any well-equipped laboratory; the Kibble balance.** NIST: the fixed value of h allows "Kibble balances to measure mass without recourse to the IPK or any physical object"; "NIST-4 has become the official U.S. standard for realizing mass — a role formerly played by K20" (K20 is the United States' copy number 20). A Kibble balance balances the weight of a mass against the force on a coil carrying a current in a magnetic field: "weighs a mass against an electromagnetic force". The other way, counting the atoms of a silicon sphere, isn't shown.
- **Told blamelessly, nothing French in the sound.** The story is the metrologists': nobody erred; the cylinder did what a definition held by one object can do. The BIPM is international; the film says "near Paris" and draws nothing national.
- **Casual teaching, claims and pay day.** As the HR system's words have them (`sources/hr_system/_hr_system__docs.md`; the module's `numbers.md`): "A casual academic gets a contract for each teaching period they teach, for one unit"; casual staff claim the hours they worked in the claims app, the unit's coordinator approves each claim, and pay day is every second Thursday. The claim card on screen shows a duty, hours and status, never an amount or a name: no person's pay on screen or in the voice.
- **The three numbers.** Agreed with *Reports on demand* and *More readers than people* on 5 November 2026 (`numbers.md`, *Semester 1 2026, the Faculty of Health*): A$2.46 million (casual hours contracted, the faculty's spreadsheet), A$1.88 million (casual pay paid on Semester 1's pay days, payroll's report), A$2.21 million (claims approved for Semester 1's teaching, with on-costs: casual teaching cost). - **The definition.** Casual teaching cost, as the module's `numbers.md` agreed it: the claims approved for a teaching period's teaching (casual staff, pay type CAS, teaching duties only), plus on-costs, counted by the teaching period the teaching was done in, not the day it was paid; owned by Rohan Desai; synonyms "sessional teaching cost" and "casual teaching spend"; by faculty, unit and teaching period. Teaching duties are tutorial, marking and consultation, as *One copy, one platform* set the claims app's duties (training isn't teaching). - **Metric views.** "Metric views are the core implementation of Unity Catalog semantics"; "With a metric view, you define the metric once ... and users can group by any available field. The query engine generates the correct computation"; read from "SQL editors, notebooks, dashboards, Genie Agents, and alerts" and from "Power BI, Tableau, Sigma, and other external BI tools" (*Unity Catalog metric views*, updated 29 October 2026). "Metric views use the same hierarchical permissions model as other Unity Catalog securable objects"; "You must be the owner of a metric view to edit its definition"; the Lineage tab shows what depends on it (*Manage metric views*, updated 29 October 2026). The film's "who may read it, and what reads it" is that.
