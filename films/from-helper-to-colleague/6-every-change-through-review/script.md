## The promise

Every line passes two tests: someone who has only heard of AI agents can follow it, and an engineering lead who runs GitHub, dbt and Databricks for a data team finds nothing wrong in it. The film is stage 4 of the series, *A contributor that proposes*, at its fullest hand-off: the planning agent's first pull request, its evidence, the checks on what changed and what depends on it, a diff of the data, the governance review agent's review for reference, the owners' approval, and a release that a second person approves. Rodrigo's practice is its spine: every change to production is approved by a second person, for agents as for people; agents are never approvers, and their review is only for reference. The film shows the rules that make that hold, where each is set: on `main` in GitHub, in `CODEOWNERS`, in the environment the release waits at, and in the bundle the release deploys to Databricks as its own service principal. *Plan, define the check, do, check* already showed Jun reviewing an agent's work and Noor approving it; this film names it and shows why it can't go any other way. How the project's checks, owners and contracts were built is *In the weeds of data crafting*'s, named by title where they appear. **An agent proposes; a person releases.**

## The story in one paragraph

Today, at the university: on Wednesday 4 November 2026, the planning agent, Planning's contributor with its own name since Monday, opened its first pull request. It adds each student's study mode, on campus or online, to Planning's census-day count, and answers the library's question of *Context and memory, governed*: the 386 who study online can't come in for a book. But that count is the one everyone reads, 18,412 students, for the board and for the government's report, and a change that dropped or doubled students would move it; an agent that could approve its own change could put one there before anyone had looked. That had to change before the agent started. So every change, an agent's or a person's, goes through review, with the rules set in the tools: the agent hands in its evidence; checks build what changed and what depends on it; a diff shows the data before and after; the governance review agent reviews it, for reference; the owners approve the code and the meaning; and a second person releases it to production. Jun, Mei and Noor saw exactly what would change, one new column and no count moved; the board's number stays 18,412, and the library has its answer, 386 online and 18,026 on campus. The film then follows the pull request: Clara's ticket and Mei's word in the glossary first, the agent's branch and its own schema, its tests and evidence (*What the agent hands in*); the checks and the diff (*What changed, and what it touches*); the governance review agent's comment, Clara's decision and the agent's second push (*An agent's review, for reference*); the rules on `main` (*Rules that hold for everyone*); Jun's merge, Noor's approval and the release as its own service principal (*A person releases*); and Thursday morning, with the lesson (*An agent proposes, a person releases*).

### 5 · Rules that hold for everyone

**Narration:** In Plan, define the check, do, check, Jun reviewed an agent's work and Noor approved it. These rules make that hold. ## Rigour to check before recording

As Rodrigo set on 5 November 2026 (10:39), the film never shows or says a feature's release status (preview, beta, generally available): not in the narration, on screen, in the captions, on its page or in Pause and think. The status read on the day is kept only in *Sources*, with its date.

- **The rules on `main` (GitHub, checked 5 November 2026).** "Pull request authors cannot approve their own pull requests." Code owners' review: "if code has multiple owners, an approval from any of the code owners will be sufficient"; code owners "must have write permissions for the repository", and are users or teams. The latest push: "Whether the most recent reviewable push must be approved by someone other than the person who pushed it" (`require_last_push_approval`). Stale approvals dismissed on a new push. The required reviewer rule: approvals from designated teams for files that match a pattern, and "the team must have write permissions". A ruleset's bypass list empty: nobody bypasses it. Several rulesets on one branch: "the rules in each of these rulesets are aggregated". So the film says an agent's approval never counts where the rules ask for a person, because owners, required teams and environment reviewers are users or teams.
- **Merging.** GitHub documents no rule that takes away merging alone from an App that can push to its own branches ("Restrict updates" is documented for pushes), so the film says the agents *don't* merge (their rules) and that merging isn't releasing; it never says they *can't*. The series' rows say the same (`numbers.md`, *What GitHub's rules can and can't say about merging*).
- **The review agent's comment.** GitHub's three ways to submit a review: "Select **Comment** to leave general feedback without explicitly approving the changes or requesting additional changes." Its App can't push (Contents: read), so it changes no file. Its stage for this task is 1: it flags and suggests.
