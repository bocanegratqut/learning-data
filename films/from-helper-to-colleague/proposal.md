## The brief

The module, in the author's words of 3 November 2026: the basic ideas first, then the journey from people using agents on their behalf to autonomous contributors, with their own identity, permissions and human owner, architected and managed as enterprise architecture, governance and security describe. Four series: *What an agent is*, *Agents at work on data*, *From helper to colleague* (this one) and *A colleague that isn't a person*.

*Agents at work on data* put the agent to work and showed it on Databricks, governed. This series is the road an agent travels as a team comes to trust it, in five stages, each with a gate: it suggests; it acts for a person, step by step; it acts for them, within limits; it becomes a contributor that proposes, with a name of its own; and, for some kinds of task, a contributor with a written mandate. Each stage changes whose identity the agent uses, how much it may do and what the person does. An agent moves up a stage only when it passes the gate, and back down after a serious incident, and its stage is set for each kind of task and each environment, not for the agent as a whole.

He agreed with this plan on 5 November 2026: "Use our project sample as the baseline for this demonstration of agents working together and refer to that module for more details on construction. From there, make the seven films".

## The reference, and its alternatives

The stages follow the levels of autonomy defined by what the person does, adapted to data work, with every change to production approved by a second person, as for people's changes. The author agreed on 3 November 2026, and the films name the frameworks in their references.

| Body of knowledge | What it adds | Here |
|---|---|---|
| Levels of autonomy for AI agents, by the person's role (Feng, McDonald and Zhang, 2025: operator, collaborator, consultant, approver, observer) | Autonomy as a design choice, set by what the person does | The five stages; 3.1 |
| Human oversight (Europe's AI law; ISO/IEC 42001, the AI management system) | People able to understand, watch and stop the system; owners, risk reviews and records, auditable | 3.1, 3.7 |
| Least privilege (Saltzer and Schroeder, 1975); acting on someone's behalf, and the confused deputy (Hardy, 1988); OWASP's excessive agency | Every program gets only the rights its job needs; a program that acts for a person can be tricked into using that person's rights | 3.2, 3.4 |
| ArchiMate 4, as *A map people and agents can read* draws an agent; actors with an autonomy level, decision rights and an escalation path (*The map before the data*) | An agent on the map as software assigned to a role, with an owner, its rights beside everyone else's | 3.3 |
| Workload identities and federation (OpenID Connect) | Software that signs in as itself, with no secret it has to keep | 3.5 |
| Separation of duties; review before release (*Change without surprises*) | Nobody approves their own change; a second person releases it to production | 3.6, 3.7 |
| Databricks', GitHub's and dbt's docs, checked on the day | How the stages are built and enforced in the tools | 3.4 to 3.7 |

Alternatives worth knowing, named once:

- **Levels of driving automation** (SAE J3016, levels 0 to 5): autonomy defined by what the machine does. The series defines it by what the person does, as Feng, McDonald and Zhang do, because in data work the person's part is what keeps the accountability.
- **Role-based and attribute-based access control**: who may do what. The series uses both (an agent's rights come from its role's group; masks follow tags), and adds that an agent's autonomy is a separate choice for each kind of task.
- **The NIST AI Risk Management Framework** (govern, map, measure, manage): named once, as a way to organise the risks this series designs for.

Where the series differs from them: an agent is a contributor with a role, never an approver, and however far it goes, a person releases every change to production.

## Practical advice the films carry

- Set an agent's stage for each kind of task and each environment, and write down the gate to the next.
- Start a helper on a person's sign-in only to learn: a development environment, read-only tools, drafts in a sandbox, no sensitive data, and the person approving each action.
- Before an agent works alone, put it on the map: a role, an owner who answers for it, rights from the role, what it may decide, and whom it asks.
- Give it an identity of its own in every tool, with no secret it can see or leak, rights from its role's group, and its own isolated environment, so its work is labelled and it can be stopped without stopping anyone else.
- Let it propose, never release: checks on what changed, a diff of the data, a code owner's review, and a second person's approval for production, with rules that stop anyone approving their own change.
- Write its autonomy down as a narrow mandate its owner signs, enforce it where the work happens, keep a switch that stops it, and make every change undoable.
- After a serious incident, move the task down a stage until the gate is passed again.
